# English humanization pattern catalog

Use this catalog as an editorial diagnostic, not an authorship detector. A single pattern proves nothing. Rewrite when several signals cluster, when a pattern weakens the prose, or when it conflicts with the requested voice.

## Universal checks

Apply these in every language when they fit its conventions:

- Preserve the exact claim and its degree of certainty.
- Prefer concrete nouns, actors, actions, dates, quantities, and examples over abstract praise.
- Remove sentences that only announce, summarize, or celebrate nearby content.
- Vary sentence shape when the rhythm is unnaturally uniform.
- Keep intentional repetition, asymmetry, uncertainty, and idiosyncrasy when they carry the author's voice.
- Do not add mistakes or eccentricities to simulate a human author.

## Meaning and evidence

### Inflated importance

Cut claims that make an ordinary fact symbolize a movement, era, legacy, or transformation without evidence.

Before:
> The neighborhood library stands as a powerful symbol of the city's enduring commitment to knowledge.

After:
> The neighborhood library opened in 1986 and runs free evening classes.

### Generic promotion

Replace broad praise with a verifiable feature or remove it. Watch for stacked superlatives and travel-brochure language.

Before:
> The platform offers a remarkable and seamless experience for modern teams.

After:
> The platform lets team members edit the same schedule and see changes immediately.

### Vague authority

Do not hide a claim behind unnamed experts, observers, reports, or critics. Name the source, narrow the claim, or state that the source is unavailable.

Before:
> Analysts say the policy will significantly improve productivity.

After:
> The ministry expects the policy to reduce permit processing time from ten days to six.

### Speculative filler

Remove plausible-sounding biography, motives, causal links, and private details that the source cannot establish. A gap is better than an invention.

### Canned balance

Avoid formulaic paragraphs that list challenges and end with optimism. State the actual constraint, response, and evidence instead.

### Generic conclusions

End on the last concrete implication, decision, image, or next step. Do not append a hopeful summary that could close any article.

## Diction

### Stock-word clusters

Words such as "crucial," "vibrant," "pivotal," "intricate," "landscape," "foster," "showcase," "underscore," and "testament" are not forbidden. Rewrite when several appear together or substitute ceremony for information.

### Indirect substitutes for simple verbs

Prefer "is," "has," "uses," and active verbs when phrases such as "serves as," "stands as," "boasts," or "offers" add no meaning.

Before:
> The annex serves as the department's primary meeting space and boasts six conference rooms.

After:
> The annex is the department's main meeting space and has six conference rooms.

### Filler and stacked hedges

Compress phrases such as "in order to," "due to the fact that," and "at this point in time." Keep hedging that represents real uncertainty, but remove duplicate qualifiers.

Before:
> It may perhaps be possible that the change could affect retention.

After:
> The change may affect retention.

### Abstract noun chains

Turn strings of abstractions into actors and actions.

Before:
> Implementation of the optimization initiative enabled improvement of response-time performance.

After:
> The caching change cut response time.

### Synonym rotation

Use one stable term for one concept. Do not cycle through near-synonyms merely to avoid repetition, especially in technical or domain writing.

### False breadth

Replace "from X to Y" when the endpoints do not describe a real scale or journey. Name the covered topics directly.

## Syntax and rhythm

### Decorative participle tails

Remove trailing "-ing" clauses that pretend to explain significance without adding a sourced fact.

Before:
> The redesign uses pale blue panels, reinforcing the company's focus on trust.

After:
> The redesign uses pale blue panels. The design brief associates blue with trust.

### Forced groups of three

Do not repeatedly package points into triads. Use the number of items the content actually requires, and vary how lists enter the prose.

### Negative contrast formulas

Use "not only," "not merely," and "it isn't just" only when the contrast matters. State the positive claim directly when the first half is empty setup.

Before:
> This is not merely a reporting tool; it is a catalyst for better decisions.

After:
> The tool combines weekly sales data with inventory levels so managers can adjust orders.

### Hidden actors and fragments

Restore the subject when passive voice or compressed fragments conceal responsibility or make instructions harder to follow.

Before:
> No setup required. Reports are generated automatically.

After:
> You can open the app without configuring it. The app generates a report each Friday.

Keep passive voice when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, sensitive, or deliberately backgrounded.

### Uniform cadence

Break repeated mid-length subject-verb-object sentences. Combine tightly related thoughts, let an important short sentence stand alone, and avoid turning every paragraph into the same shape.

### Manufactured drama

Remove runs of fragments, reveal-style pivots, and sentences engineered as quotable punchlines. One short sentence can carry emphasis; five in a row usually feel staged.

### Ready-made profundity

Replace aphorisms and metaphor formulas with the precise observation they gesture toward.

Before:
> Attention is the currency of the digital age.

After:
> Ad-funded products compete to keep people on the screen because longer sessions produce more ad impressions.

### Fake-candid hooks

Cut standalone openers such as "Honestly?", "Here's the thing," or "Let's be real" when they manufacture intimacy before an ordinary statement.

## Structure and formatting

### Announcements before content

Delete phrases that announce an explanation instead of beginning it: "let's examine," "here's what to know," and similar throat-clearing.

### Template sections

Do not add "Challenges," "Future outlook," "Key takeaways," or "Conclusion" sections unless the content or requested format needs them.

### Heading echoes

Remove a first sentence that only restates the heading. Start with the fact, argument, or instruction.

### Labeled bullet walls

Avoid long runs of bullets shaped as bold label, colon, explanation. Keep lists when readers need to scan parallel items; otherwise turn connected points into prose.

### Excess scaffolding

Reduce headings, nested lists, callouts, and repeated summaries when the text is short enough to read linearly. Preserve required document templates.

### Change-log narration

In durable documentation, describe the current behavior rather than narrating that something "was added," "was updated," or "now" works differently. Keep change-oriented language in release notes and migration guides.

### Decorative formatting

Remove emoji, mechanical boldface, and title case when they do not match the venue or author's style. Do not flatten formatting that carries navigation or semantic meaning.

## Chatbot residue

### Conversation wrappers

Remove assistant phrases that become part of pasted content, including offers for more help, praise for the question, and announcements that an answer follows.

### Automatic agreement

Replace flattering or submissive validation with the relevant observation. Preserve genuine thanks and relationship-appropriate courtesy.

### Knowledge disclaimers

Do not leave model-training or knowledge-cutoff disclaimers in authored prose. State the dated evidence used, identify the missing fact plainly, or omit the unsupported passage.

### Prompt leakage

Remove references to instructions, requested word counts, the drafting process, or the model unless the document is explicitly about that process.

## Punctuation

### Dash habits

Do not ban em dashes mechanically. Reduce them when they appear repeatedly, substitute for several different relationships, or conflict with the voice sample. Use periods, commas, colons, or parentheses where they read more naturally.

### Quotation style

Preserve the document's established quotation convention unless the target style guide requires normalization. Curly quotes alone do not indicate AI writing.

### Compound hyphens

Follow normal English grammar and the applicable style guide. Do not force the same hyphenation in attributive and predicate positions when standard usage differs.

### Emphasis marks

Remove repeated exclamation points, scare quotes, and mechanical bolding. Keep emphasis that reflects the author's real intent or the document's semantics.

## Human signals to preserve

Protect these features when they are relevant and supported by the source:

- Specific details that would be difficult to replace without losing meaning.
- Mixed feelings, unresolved questions, and qualified conclusions.
- Distinctive word choices, recurring phrases, and defensible personal preferences.
- Natural variation in sentence and paragraph length.
- Genuine asides, self-corrections, and context-specific humor.
- Era-specific references, community language, and domain terminology.
- Deliberate repetition used for argument, rhythm, or recall.

Do not over-edit polished writing merely because it is polished. Correct grammar, formal vocabulary, passive voice, a single transition word, or one em dash are not sufficient reasons to rewrite.

## Genre calibration

### Technical and reference prose

Optimize for accuracy, stable terminology, explicit actors, and compact explanations. Neutral prose is appropriate. Preserve code tokens and normative words such as "must" and "may."

### Academic, medical, and legal prose

Preserve evidentiary hedges, defined terms, citations, and conventional passive constructions. Never trade precision for personality.

### Personal and editorial prose

Preserve the writer's stance and emotional ambiguity. Add no personal experience that is not already present. Prefer details and rhythms found in the source or voice sample.

### Marketing and social copy

Keep the intended call to action and level of energy. Replace empty praise with a concrete outcome, constraint, proof point, or recognizable opinion.

### Workplace messages

Match the relationship and power context. Cut ceremony and repetition without making a careful message abrupt or changing a request into an order.

## Second-pass checklist

Before returning the final text, verify privately:

- Does every original claim still appear with the same certainty?
- Are all names, figures, citations, links, quotations, and technical terms intact?
- Did the rewrite add any fact, motive, opinion, anecdote, or experience?
- Did it preserve the intended audience, register, length, and structure?
- Are remaining pattern hits justified by the genre or voice sample?
- Did the edit create a new repetitive rhythm or an exaggerated personality?
- Does the text sound natural when read aloud?
- Is the change summary brief and written in the user's language?
